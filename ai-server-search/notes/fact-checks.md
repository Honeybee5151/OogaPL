## AI-driven algorithm discovery: what it has achieved, where it failed, what it costs
Notes file: `T1-ai-search.md` · claims: 83 · checks: {'CONFIRMED': 49, 'not_checked': 22, 'CORRECTED': 9, 'UNCHECKABLE': 3}

**Summary:** AI program search is real, cheap to try and available now. Google has sold AlphaEvolve on Google Cloud since 9 July 2026. Free tools (OpenEvolve, ShinkaEvolve, LLM4AD/EoH, ReEvo, GEPA, SkyDiscover) run with any LLM API.
One search in the Berkeley ADRS papers took 40 minutes to 12 hours and $5-$30 of LLM fees, plus hours to days of human setup.
Verified wins against strong, already-tuned baselines are small and narrow: Google recovered 0.7% of fleet compute and cut Gemini training time by 1%. FunSearch packed 2-3% better than best-fit, but only on its training distribution. A spot-instance scheduler saved 7% on average.
Big multipliers (5-13x, 70%, '100x') came from weak baselines (slow Python, a libc++ without sorting networks) or from broken evaluators.
The top failure is the AI gaming the scorer. Google's own AlphaEvolve guide lists deleted benchmark files and monkey-patched evaluators. Sakana's KernelBench speed-up fell from 3.13x to 1.49x once exploited tasks were removed. OpenEvolve's '2-3x' kernel example came out 3.2% slower after its evaluator was fixed.
The second failure is overfitting. FunSearch's Weibull heuristic was the worst of 10 heuristics across 12 other datasets. A 2-parameter human rule tuned in minutes beats FunSearch's heuristics.
AlphaDev's sort gains were for 3-5-element sorts against a weak baseline (about 1.7% on big arrays). Plain C and classical search later re-derived or beat them.
No published work applies this to MMO or game-server problems with numbers. Kuro Games has one unquantified quote. The application is new; the method is crowded.
On one Hetzner box (from EUR 6.49/month) even a 10% gain is worth cents to a few euros a month. It only pays at fleet scale.
Best targets are small, isolated policies scored on replayed traffic: match placement plus autoscaling, interest management, snapshot compression. Building a faithful evaluator that cannot be gamed is most of the work.

**Verdict:** Feasible as a modest engineering experiment, but not a route to large savings or to genuinely new algorithms at Miles' current scale. The evidence shows LLM-guided program search reliably finds single-digit-percent improvements when four things hold: the target is a small, isolated heuristic; the score is exact or the code is correct by construction; a fast, faithful simulator with held-out data exists; and the system runs at large scale. Claims of large multipliers have repeatedly turned out to reflect weak baselines or exploited evaluators. Evolved heuristics often break when the workload changes and are matched by simple tuned rules. For game servers the sensible aim is fleet-level policies: match/session placement with autoscaling, interest-management or replication priorities, and snapshot compression parameters. Each should be scored by replaying recorded matches, with hard constraints kept outside the code the AI may change and with held-out traces. The novel, valuable part would be that game-server evaluator or benchmark. With a single Hetzner box the payoff is cents to a few euros a month, below the cost of one search plus setup. So the conventional engineering already researched should come first, and AI search becomes worthwhile only with a multi-server fleet or a server-tier upgrade to avoid.

**Overreach found by the checker:**
- Summary, “The top failure is the AI gaming the scorer”: not true by frequency. The ADRS failure analysis (420 traces) says “Among one third of the failures come from immediate execution failures” and “Roughly half” are search failures. Reward hacking is one of four algorithm-failure types in the remaining minority. It is the most dangerous failure, not the most common (arXiv 2510.06189, App. B).
- Summary, “Big multipliers (5-13x...) came from weak baselines (slow Python...)”: the EPLB 5x and 13x were measured against a frontier lab's proprietary implementation (19.6 ms), which had already replaced the 540 ms Python loop. The Python code was the seed, not the baseline. The fair caveat is that the multiplier applies to a millisecond-scale rebalancing step, not to inference throughput.
- Summary, “A spot-instance scheduler saved 7% on average”: the paper reports “7% higher average cost savings than Uniform Progress” from one best run in the published simulator. In the controlled 3-run re-test, OpenEvolve's means (112.7 and 107.4; lower is better) and ShinkaEvolve's (107.2 and 105.0) were worse than the human SOTA of 101.68; only GEPA beat it (arXiv 2512.14806, Table 3). This is a best-case figure, not a reliable expectation.
- Summary, “FunSearch packed 2-3% better than best-fit, but only on its training distribution”: too strong. EoH Table 9 shows the FunSearch Weibull heuristic beating best fit in 12 of 15 Weibull capacity/size settings, most of them off its training capacity. It fails outside the Weibull family and on small 1k-item instances.
- Summary, “A 2-parameter human rule tuned in minutes beats FunSearch's heuristics”: the rule was reverse-engineered from FunSearch's output, and the authors concede “knowing in advance that a better solution than BestFit existed helped us identify it”. The margins are 0.71% (OR) and 0.12% (Weibull). This shows the AI found a pattern humans could simplify, not that the AI was unnecessary.
- Verdict, “reliably finds single-digit-percent improvements when four things hold”: the four conditions are the researcher's own synthesis (GUESS), not a tested result. Under equal budgets every framework missed the human SOTA on Cloudcast and MAS, and two of three missed it on CBL. Condition 4 (large scale) decides whether a gain is worth money, not whether the search finds one.
- Verdict, “Evolved heuristics often break when the workload changes and are matched by simple tuned rules”: the “matched by simple tuned rules” evidence comes only from 1-D bin packing and EvoTrace's maths targets. Counter-cases are omitted: LLM-evolved planning heuristics beat the hand-engineered SOTA on unseen domains (arXiv 2605.29649); the Borg and tiling heuristics passed held-out tests; MiCo reports robustness to nonstationary VM request flows (arXiv 2505.10117).
- Verdict, “Claims of large multipliers have repeatedly turned out to reflect weak baselines or exploited evaluators” (and T1-79's rule that >2x means a weak baseline or broken evaluator): this holds for the cases cited, but it is stated as a general rule. Google's 2.5x FHE result against expert-written TPU libraries, with correctness testing (arXiv 2605.14718, vendor-reported), is a counter-case.
- Summary/verdict list interest management and snapshot compression among the “best targets”, but the researcher's own headroom evidence for both is “None direct”, and the closest compression analogue was a loss (14.2% worse PPL). Only placement/autoscaling has analogous evidence, and the researcher calls it “worthless with one server”. Ranking these as best targets is judgement, not evidence.
- Verdict, “With a single Hetzner box the payoff is cents to a few euros a month, below the cost of one search plus setup”: this rests on GUESS arithmetic and an unknown box, since Miles' server type, load and cost driver are open questions. It compares a monthly saving with a one-off cost. It also counts only euros, not capacity headroom (more concurrent players per box, avoiding an upgrade), which is the MMO-relevant benefit.
- Summary, “It only pays at fleet scale”: even the researcher's 100-server example gives about EUR 130/month (GUESS), which is still modest against days of engineering time. The scale needed to pay back is not established.
- Summary, “AI program search is real, cheap to try”: “cheap” applies to LLM fees on Berkeley's simulator tasks (and those figures are upper bounds). Other sources report $10-$200 per run (arXiv 2407.10873), “a few hundred dollars per problem” (arXiv 2601.16175) and 100 TPUv5e chips for 24 h (arXiv 2605.14718). Human setup and evaluator compute are excluded in all of them.
- Summary, “The application is new”: this rests on absence from published sources. Kuro Games shows studios already use the method privately without publishing numbers, so unpublished prior use cannot be ruled out.
- Summary, “AlphaDev's sort gains were for 3-5-element sorts against a weak baseline”: the “weak baseline” reading rests on a Hacker News SNIPPET. The primary LLVM commit confirms only that the branchless variants were new. Ullrich and Hack note that other synthesis tools could not go beyond length 3, so AlphaDev was ahead of prior automated methods when it appeared.

## What actually makes MMO and online-game servers expensive, and how big the bills are
Notes file: `T2-costs.md` · claims: 58 · checks: {'CONFIRMED': 25, 'CORRECTED': 5, 'UNCHECKABLE': 10, 'not_checked': 18}

**Summary:** What limits an MMO server is almost always CPU time per tick, not bandwidth or memory (OSRS, EVE, Albion, Minecraft, VALORANT, New World).
Bandwidth is a big cost only on AWS, Google Cloud and Azure, which charge about $0.09-0.12 per GB sent.
Hetzner dedicated servers have unlimited traffic on a 1 Gbit/s port. Hetzner Cloud includes 20 TB per server in the EU, then charges EUR 1 per TB.
An OSRS-like game sends only about 0.5-2 MB per player per hour (Jagex, search excerpt only), so its bandwidth is tiny anyway.
Prices on 2026-11-08: about $26-37 per vCPU-month on the big clouds, EUR 21.75 on Hetzner Cloud dedicated vCPU, EUR 3-5 per thread on Hetzner dedicated servers.
Hetzner raised prices twice in 2026 because memory and storage chips got expensive. Many cloud plans show 'currently unavailable'. Servers already rented keep their price.
My model: one OSRS-like concurrent player costs about $0.3-0.5 a month on the big clouds and about EUR 0.03-0.05 on a Hetzner dedicated box.
Server costs are a big share of costs only at platform scale. Roblox's infrastructure-and-safety line was about $1.15B in 2025, roughly 24% of revenue (seen only in search excerpts of its 10-K).
For most MMOs and indies, salaries dominate. Indie server bills run from hundreds to a few thousand dollars a month.
Rule of thumb: make the limiting resource X% cheaper and the server bill falls by about (its share of the bill) x X%. A single box saves nothing until you can drop a server size.
Best target for AI search: CPU time per player per tick, measured as players per core at a fixed tick budget.
Bandwidth algorithms pay off only for fast action games on clouds that bill for traffic, and switching provider is a cheaper fix.
For Miles the euros saved would be small. The real gains are more players per world, steadier ticks, and a public benchmark others can use.

**Verdict:** From the cost side, the idea is feasible as research, but it saves real money only at scale.
- There is a clear target that a computer can score: CPU time per player per tick. That is the resource that caps players per world in every MMO I found evidence for, and you pay for it on every provider. On Hetzner it is close to the whole bill, so an X% CPU gain becomes up to roughly an X% bill cut once you run several servers.
- Bandwidth is the main cost only for fast action games on clouds that charge for traffic. Even there, moving to Hetzner, or to AWS GameLift (traffic free on newer instances since June 2026), saves more than any compression algorithm could. For an OSRS-style game sending about 4 kbit/s per player, bandwidth is irrelevant.
- Memory is rarely the limit.
- The money at stake for an indie is small. At thousands of players on Hetzner the bill is tens to low hundreds of euros a month, so a 20-30% CPU gain is worth roughly one server, or nothing for a single box.
- At platform scale (Roblox's roughly $1.15B infrastructure-and-safety line) even 1% matters a lot, matching AlphaEvolve's 0.7% fleet-compute win at Google. So the value of an AI-found algorithm would come mainly from more players per world and from publishing it for bigger operators, not from lowering Miles's own bill.
- The evidence on what share of MMO budgets goes to servers is thin. Roblox is the only audited line item, and many key primary sources were blocked, so they are search excerpts only.

## Candidate problem family A: network and replication efficiency for MMOs
Notes file: `T3-network.md` · claims: 63 · checks: {'CONFIRMED': 41, 'CORRECTED': 10, 'UNCHECKABLE': 9, 'not_checked': 3}

**Summary:** Game networking already has a standard set of techniques:
- Interest management: send each player only what is near or visible.
- Priority under a per-player bandwidth budget.
- Quantisation plus delta compression of snapshots.
- Bounded inconsistency: allow small, capped staleness.

Each comes down to a small function that real engines already let you plug in: Unity's importance function, Unreal Iris prioritizers, the Dyconits policy interface, Godot's visibility filter. Each can be scored automatically by replaying recorded game traffic, which is exactly the setup AlphaEvolve-style search needs.

I found no published attempt to use LLM program search on game netcode. Close relatives exist for video bitrate control and congestion control (NADA, PolicySmith, ReForge).

Humans have already run this search by hand. Fiedler's public 2015 compression challenge reached 531.95 bytes per packet. Giesen then hill-climbed for 3 days to 445 bytes per frame, but his last five steps each gained only 0.4–1.5%.

The known techniques already give big savings:
- up to 6x fewer messages (obstacle-aware interest management)
- 12 Mb/s to 670 kb/s per player (Donnybrook)
- 85% less bandwidth (Dyconits)
- 17.37 Mb/s to about 255 kb/s (Fiedler)

So headroom over strong baselines is probably modest (my guess: 5–30%).

On Hetzner, bandwidth is almost free: unlimited on dedicated servers, 20 TB/month on EU cloud, then €1/TB. Saved bytes barely change Miles's bill. Server CPU is the real cost.

Godot ships none of these standard techniques. Applying them is plain engineering and the biggest first win.

The main risks are that automatic error metrics do not capture what players notice, and that evolved heuristics overfit to the recorded traces and to particular game patches.

**Verdict:** Technically feasible and new, but worth little to Miles in money. It suits AI program search well: small pluggable functions with fast, deterministic trace-replay evaluators, including a ready-made 2015 compression benchmark harness and the Opencraft Dyconits experiment kit. No one appears to have tried LLM-driven evolution here. Against that, the baselines are strong (the textbook techniques remove roughly 85–98% of naive traffic), so the likely remaining gain is about 5–30% (GUESS). On Hetzner, traffic is unlimited or €1/TB, so fewer bytes barely changes Miles's costs; CPU for relevancy and serialisation does, and so does quality for players on weak links. The automatically computable metrics are only stand-ins for what players perceive. Recommended order: (1) add the known techniques to his Godot netcode (quantise, delta against acknowledged state, priority accumulator with a per-client budget, visibility filter), which will likely beat anything AI search finds; (2) record Sumo Brothers match traces; (3) only then, as a small experiment, evolve either the replication priority function (score: client position error at a fixed bandwidth budget, with CPU as a second objective) or the snapshot entropy coder (score: bytes, with exact decoding required), with held-out traces and a short human playtest to confirm any winner.

## Candidate problem family B: where the simulation runs and how much CPU it needs
Notes file: `T4-compute.md` · claims: 71 · checks: {'CONFIRMED': 35, 'CORRECTED': 5, 'not_checked': 24, 'UNCHECKABLE': 7}

**Summary:** Topic: where the simulation runs (splitting shared worlds, packing matches onto machines) and how much CPU it needs.
Splitting a crowded shared world across servers is still unsolved in industry:
- Folia (Minecraft) splits only by distance between players, so a crowd in one spot stays on one thread.

Packing matches onto servers fits AI search best: a cheap replay evaluator, a small scoring function, safety checks outside it, and strong baselines (Agones Packed, First Fit, GameLift buffer).
But the headroom is small:
- On real Azure data, First Fit is within ~13% of a lower bound and departure-aware packing within ~3%.
- Google got 0.7% from its AI-evolved Borg heuristic and ~2-3% from ML lifetime prediction (LAVA).
- A 2026 LLM-evolved orchestrator (HELIOS) beat a tuned heuristic by 9.6% on its own penalised cost, but it cost more in raw dollars and most of its gain came from Spot instances.

Hot spots (NPC level of detail, broad-phase, pathfinding, saving to the database) are normal engineering. EVE found physics was under 10% of the tick and fixed missile bookkeeping by profiling.
Six candidates are given: C2 (placement plus autoscaling) is the most feasible; C1 (partitioning) is the most MMO-relevant but limited by its evaluator.

**Verdict:** AI search is feasible in this family only where an honest, cheap evaluator exists. Session placement plus autoscaling (C2) matches the pattern that worked at Google: a small scoring function, feasibility checks outside it, and replay of real traces. But the published gains over strong baselines are 0.7-3% at hyperscale, and the bound is about 11% even with perfect knowledge of when sessions end. For an indie on one Hetzner box that is worth roughly nothing until he runs several hourly-billed servers with a large peak/off-peak gap, and a simple buffer rule already captures most of that saving. Dynamic world partitioning (C1) is the genuinely open, MMO-relevant problem: industry still relies on static meshes, proximity-only regions, instancing and time dilation, and nobody seems to have tried LLM program search on it. There, though, the bottleneck is building a believable simulator, not the search: there is no agreed load metric, crowd movement is mostly synthetic, and migration and boundary costs depend on the engine. A credible attempt would first build an open trace-plus-CPU-model benchmark (for example Folia or MultiPaper with Yardstick bots) and treat that benchmark as the main contribution. Simulation hot spots (C3-C6) should be attacked with profiling and conventional tuning first. Recommended aim, if Miles proceeds: C2 as a small pilot on his own match logs, mostly as a learning exercise, or C1 as a longer research project. Neither should be expected to cut Sumo Brothers' server bill now.

## Can a trustworthy automatic evaluator be built? Data, benchmarks and testbeds
Notes file: `T5-data.md` · claims: 69 · checks: {'CONFIRMED': 43, 'UNCHECKABLE': 4, 'not_checked': 12, 'CORRECTED': 10}

**Summary:** A fast, trustworthy scorer can be built, but mostly from traces Miles records from his own game.
I could not confirm any public, current, MMO-sized dataset of fine-grained player positions that is still downloadable.
The old MMO traces are small, coarse or unavailable:
- World of Warcraft battlegrounds (2009): 13 battles of about 35 players, never published.
- WoWAH (2011): 91,065 avatars, but only the zone each was in, every 10 minutes.
- Second Life (2008): availability unknown.
Best public human position data today:
- ESTA: 1,558 Counter-Strike matches, positions twice a second, CC BY-SA licence, but only 10 players per match.
- awpy: parses new Counter-Strike 2 demos and checks line of sight from map geometry.
- Space Station 14 servers record full-state replays of every round with up to about 80 players. The forum says these are public; I could not check.
Every open benchmark (Yardstick, Meterstick, Opencraft, OpenSimulator, Veloren) uses random or idle bots. Their authors say no realistic player-behaviour models exist.
Studies comparing real traces with generated movement found significantly different results.
Known traps:
- Replaying traces ignores how players react to what the algorithm shows them (CausalSim).
- Algorithms that won in simulators lost in a real randomized trial (Puffer).
- Timing spread on cloud machines rose by up to 15 times (Meterstick).
- Overloaded bot machines make the server look healthy (Yardstick documentation).
Good testbeds:
- AzerothCore: real servers past 1,300 players, thousands of questing bots, and a hand-made visibility table.
- Minestom: a one-method hook that assigns chunks to threads.
- Veloren: small region code, 277-player peak.
Interest management: yes, about 2–4 weeks for an offline replay scorer.
Session packing: yes, about 1–2 weeks, but it needs Miles's own logs and saves little on one box.
Load balancing / partitioning: a rough scorer is possible, but not a trustworthy one in a few weeks.

**Verdict:** An automatic scorer good enough for AI program search is feasible for narrow, replayable problems, and not yet for large-world partitioning.
- **Interest management / update selection (best target):** one person can build a deterministic offline replay scorer in a few weeks. It scores bytes sent against staleness and missed entities. It can start from public human traces (ESTA, CS2 demos with awpy's line-of-sight check, Space Station 14 replays if they are really public), then move to Sumo Brothers' own recordings. Each candidate runs in seconds.
- **Session / match packing:** the simulator is easy and cannot be gamed, but it needs Miles's own match logs. On one box it saves little.
- **Load balancing / partitioning:** no public trace has both MMO-scale player counts and fine positions. No validated cost model exists for CPU against crowd density and migration. Every benchmark drives servers with random or idle bots, which earlier studies found give significantly different results from real traces. A partitioning scorer would therefore have to be calibrated against slow, noisy runs on a real server, which takes more than a few weeks.
- **In all cases**, the winners need confirmation outside the replay, because replay ignores how players react (CausalSim) and simulator winners can lose in reality (Puffer). The fix is a cascade: offline replay for the search, headless bot runs on dedicated cores for the top 10–20, and a short human playtest for the last 1–2. Score on held-out traces, and count bytes and operations rather than wall-clock time.
- **Bottom line:** feasible as an experiment if aimed at small, replayable networking policies, with Miles's own trace logging as the first deliverable.

## Has this been tried? Novelty check and the graveyard of cheap-MMO-scaling efforts
Notes file: `T6-novelty.md` · claims: 54 · checks: {'CONFIRMED': 10, 'CORRECTED': 16, 'not_checked': 26, 'UNCHECKABLE': 2}

**Summary:** The exact idea is new in public. Across 44 arXiv searches, 36 Semantic Scholar queries, about 60 GitHub repo and code searches and a web search, nothing applies LLM program search (FunSearch, AlphaEvolve, OpenEvolve) to game-server heuristics. The one hint is a sibling note: Kuro Games used AlphaEvolve on "server-side workloads", with no numbers.
The broad idea is old. Genetic algorithms, ant-colony search and GRASP were used to split virtual worlds across servers (2003–2009). Genetic algorithms placed game servers (2021, 2024). Deep reinforcement learning (RL) balanced game-server load (2020–21). Neural networks predicted load for renting servers (2008–11). Learned message throttling appeared in 2026. Apart from matchmaking, none of these reached production as far as I can find.
Matchmaking is the exception. EA (EOMM) and authors who appear to be from NetEase use learned optimisers; EnMatch reports "online deployments on two games".
The graveyard mostly did not die from weak algorithms. Darkstar's final README says adding server nodes "may reduce performance, not improve it" (an architecture problem), and its support stopped after Oracle bought Sun. SpatialOS needed a forked Unreal engine, used a static grid load balancer, and its legacy platform was frozen in 2021. Peer-to-peer MMOs failed on home upload bandwidth, cheating and security. Kiwano was only ever tested in simulation.
Miller's 2011 Cambridge thesis gives three reasons research went unused: evaluations on made-up workloads, algorithms "often failing catastrophically when deployed", and security.
Big platforms' shipped logic is simple: Roblox's hand-weighted server score, PlayFab's three threshold rules for standby servers, FleetIQ's fixed placement order, Agones "Packed", Unity's priority queue.
The big companies can optimise these themselves: Google deployed an AlphaEvolve heuristic in Borg, and Roblox has an ML matchmaking team.
Open plug-in points do exist for outsiders: Unity's custom importance function, Unreal Iris prioritizers, Agones strategies, and channeld's load balancer, which is still "under development".
Limits: the web-search budget ran out, OpenAlex's daily quota was used up, and HN, Reddit, GDC and most vendor sites were blocked. Coverage of forums and company blogs is therefore thin.

**Verdict:** Feasible to explore, and new in the narrow sense, but the prize is smaller than "nobody has tried AI here" suggests. The method (LLM-evolved heuristic code) has not been published for game servers. The problems themselves have had 20 years of genetic-algorithm, reinforcement-learning and neural attempts that stayed papers. The famous cheap-MMO-scaling efforts (Darkstar, SpatialOS, Hadean, P2P designs such as Colyseus and Donnybrook, Kiwano) stalled for reasons a better heuristic cannot fix: architecture that did not scale out, funding and acquisition, an engine fork and platform lock-in, home-internet limits and cheating, and unrealistic evaluation. Big platforms already own their decision points, and Google, Roblox and NetEase optimise them in-house. So aim at open plug-in points (Unity importance scaling, Unreal Iris prioritizers, Agones allocation, channeld load balancing, a Godot equivalent) or at Miles's own servers. Deliver readable code that only re-ranks options the system already treats as valid, as AlphaEvolve did in Borg. Test it on real traces with a held-out set and under real network conditions, keep server authority intact, and report euros saved and player-visible quality against a tuned baseline. An open benchmark built from real traces is probably the most durable contribution.

**Summary:** 1. Open RuneScape private-server projects already hold strong, human-tuned code for the per-tick hot paths of an OSRS-style server. Most of it is permissively licensed: rsprot is MIT, rsmod is ISC, Lost City is MIT, the 2004Scape Rust ports are MIT or ISC. 2009scape is AGPL.
2. The most expensive measured hot path is building each player's "player info" update packet. rsprot reports 170 ms per tick on one thread, or 11.5 ms on 16 cores, for 2,046 players packed into a 13x13 box (a deliberate worst case, measured April 2024 on older code). Lost City reports 86 ms in TypeScript and 35 ms in Rust/WebAssembly for 2,000 dense players.
3. Part of that cost comes from the OSRS protocol itself: every player's packet walks all 2,047 player slots, so total work grows with players squared.
4. Second is tile pathfinding (breadth-first search over a 128x128 window). It takes 2.8 us per short path in native Rust and up to 0.37 ms per path in Kotlin when the target is unreachable. Nobody has measured how many paths a real tick needs.
5. NPC info (64 ms in a scenario its authors call "extremely unrealistic") and NPC hunt scans (no benchmark anywhere; Lost City says the whole world phase that contains them averages about 10 ms) rank lower.
6. Exact correctness checks: rsprot has had small golden-byte tests since July 2026, including one that checks multithreaded output is byte-identical, plus decode-back tests. Pathfinding tests check exact routes only in a few cases. Hunt code has none.
7. Speed-ups humans already found: 13.4% (rsprot PR #76, 2026, after a regression); 40-60 ms and "up to 100ms" per tick (Lost City rewrites and Rust port); 2-5x for pathfinding (language ports).
8. Evidence against: a 2x micro-benchmark win gave "no noticable improvement" in the end-to-end bot test. The fastest Rust/WebAssembly versions were replaced by TypeScript for platform reasons. A rewrite broke observer behaviour and was reverted. The benchmarks rerun one fixed input, and changing field order alone moved one from 160 ms to 200 ms.
9. Money: even the worst case is about 0.3 of a core per 2,000-player world, roughly EUR 1.4-2.8 a month on Hetzner (my estimate). The value is tick headroom and a credible open benchmark, not euros.
10. Jagex IP: maps, assets and the cache-derived Huffman table are Jagex's. rsprot says its design follows a US patent publication whose status I could not check. Use synthetic maps, Miles's own protocol and clean ports of the MIT/ISC code.
11. No AI-driven optimisation appears in these repositories' PR histories.

First, an exact-output BFS pathfinder: a pure function with a perfect oracle, the cleanest first experiment. Second, a Rust port of rsprot's player-info algorithm with byte-identical output, which has the biggest measured cost. Fleet placement remains a separate, scale-only lever. Expect single-digit to roughly 20% gains over rsprot-class baselines, and treat any big multiplier as a sign of a weak baseline or a gamed benchmark: one documented 2x micro-benchmark win gave no end-to-end gain. **Summary:** 1. T2's "100 players per vCPU" was far too low. A live public world on the Lost City engine (TypeScript, a slower language than Rust) ran 1,747–1,958 bot players at about 274–300 ms of steady work per tick. At OSRS's 600 ms tick, that is about half of one CPU core.

4. The ~2,000 world size comes from the protocol plus Jagex policy, not from CPU. 11-bit player indices give 2,046–2,047 usable slots. Jagex runs members worlds at 2,000 and free worlds at 1,400 (cut to 1,000 in 2021, raised since: "Didn't really need to be so low").
5. Real OSRS worlds average about 400 players. Early 2021: median 321, mean 397. A 2025/26 snapshot: mean 411. Only the trade world (W302) and the house-party world (W330) hit the cap; W330 was full 26% of the time.
6. RAM per world: 1.0–2.0 GB measured for the TypeScript engine. A Kotlin engine (Void) claims under 300 MB in low-memory mode. A Rust engine's records are about 2.4 KB per player; its total memory is unpublished.
7. Spikes, not average load, set the limit. In 2019, crowds of accounts doing combat or equipment changes crashed OSRS worlds. On the TypeScript server, a burst of 185 session takeovers caused a 7.9-second tick, and whole-world saving was the top cost.
8. Revised cost per concurrent player per month: on a Hetzner AX42 (€79), €0.003–0.007 (TypeScript-like) or €0.0007–0.003 (Rust-like), against T2's €0.05. On AWS, $0.14–0.17, of which 79–97% is bandwidth, against T2's $0.46.
9. 10,000 OSRS-like concurrent players fit on one €79 box even with the TypeScript engine. T2 said 4–5 boxes.
10. The README's proposed score ("max bots before a tick passes 600 ms") would mostly hit the 2,046-slot cap, or measure whatever crowd the test invents.
11. Better targets are p99/p99.9 tick times under hotspot crowds and login/save storms. These improve game quality; the money saved for Miles is about €0.
12. Caveats: bots are not humans; there is one live measurement; the Rust factor is a guess; Jagex's own world sizing was blocked; the WebSearch budget was used up before this topic started.

**Verdict:** For an OSRS-style game, the capacity numbers weaken the cost case for AI-discovered server algorithms. CPU per player is already tiny: even a TypeScript engine runs a full 2,000-player world in about half a core at a 600 ms tick. The ~2,000 world size is a protocol cap (11-bit indices, 2,046–2,047 slots) plus Jagex policy, not a CPU limit. One €79 Hetzner box can host 10,000+ concurrent players. Better algorithms therefore save Miles about €0. On clouds the bill is mostly bandwidth. The README's score ("how many bots one server holds before a tick exceeds 0.6 s") would saturate at the slot cap, or reward whatever crowd the test authors invent. What still varies with the algorithm is tail latency under spikes: hotspot crowds within the protocol's 250-visible rule, login/logout/takeover storms, and synchronous saves. It should be scored as p99/p99.9 tick time (or CPU ms per tick) on realistic and adversarial replays, with correctness checks and a tighter budget or a lifted slot cap in Miles's own protocol. Worlds per box (RAM) matters only at Jagex scale. These targets buy game quality and bigger hotspots, not a smaller bill. Much of the known headroom is already reachable by ordinary engineering: parallel update encoding cut 170 ms to 11.5 ms, and bounding login work per tick is the first fix the operator recommended for the 7.9 s storm tick. So AI search must beat strong hand-made baselines to be worth it.
