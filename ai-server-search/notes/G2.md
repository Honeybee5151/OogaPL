Research date: 2026-11-08. Written for Miles (Sumo Brothers; next project is an Old School RuneScape-like MMO with a 0.6 s tick).
Topic: T2's OSRS-like cost model used "100 players per vCPU" and "2,000–3,000 CCU per box", both pure guesses. This note looks for measured numbers: CPU and RAM per world, normal versus crowded play, and whether the ~2,000-player world size is a protocol cap or a CPU limit.

## 0. Plain-language summary

1. **T2's guess was far too pessimistic, by roughly 10–40× for normal play, even for a slow engine.** The range depends on how much spare capacity you keep and on how a cloud vCPU compares to a full core. I found real measurements from a public live server. It runs the Lost City engine (TypeScript, a slower language than Rust) with about 1,750–1,960 bot players and about 8,300 NPCs on one world. Its main game work took about 274–300 ms per tick. At OSRS's 600 ms tick, a full 2,000-player world would need **about half of one CPU core**. At 600–1,300 players it needs about 9–26% of a core. T2 assumed 100 players per vCPU, which would mean 20 vCPUs for 2,000 players.
2. **The ~2,000-player world size is a protocol and design cap, not a CPU limit.**
   - The RuneScape protocol numbers players with 11 bits, so a world has 2,048 slots. Slot 0 is reserved, which leaves 2,047 usable slots in the OSRS library and 2,046 in the 2004-era engines.
   - Jagex sets the operating cap below that: 2,000 on members worlds and 1,400 on free worlds.
   - Jagex cut free worlds to 1,000 in 2021 after bug abuse. A Jagex moderator said in 2025 the free-world cap "didn't really need to be so low". So the cap is a policy setting.
3. **Most real OSRS worlds are far from full.** In about 3.3 million five-minute samples from early 2021, the median world held 321 players and the mean 397. Only the house-party world (W330) and the trade world (W302) regularly reached the cap. W330 was full in 26% of samples. A late-2025/2026 snapshot looks the same: 317 worlds, mean 411, one world full.
4. **Crowds and "storms" set the limit, not average load.** The game loop of one world runs on one thread, so the busiest moment in the busiest world decides whether a tick runs late.
   - A synthetic worst case puts all 2,046 players in one 13×13-tile box, teleporting and chatting every tick. In the RSProt benchmark, building player and NPC updates for it takes about 234 ms of one core on the JVM. That is 39% of a 600 ms tick, and it spreads almost perfectly over 16 cores.
   - On real OSRS, crowds of accounts doing expensive actions have crashed worlds (2019).
   - On the live Lost City-based server, the worst spike was not a crowd. It was a burst of 185 session takeovers that produced a **7.9-second tick**. The steady top cost was saving the whole world to its database.
5. **RAM per world:**
   - TypeScript engine: about 1.0 GB at about 600–900 players, about 1.5 GB at about 1,300, and about 1.7–2.0 GB at about 1,750–1,960 (measured process memory).
   - A Kotlin engine (Void) claims "under 300MB in low-memory mode".
   - A Rust engine's per-entity records are tiny (about 2.4 KB per player). Its total memory was not published.
   - On a 64 GB Hetzner box, RAM and CPU become similar limits once the engine is efficient.
6. **Revised cost (GUESS):**
   - Hetzner AX42 (€79): about €0.003–0.007 per CCU-month for a TypeScript-like engine and €0.0007–0.003 for a Rust-like engine. T2 said €0.05.
   - AWS on-demand: about $0.14–0.17 per CCU-month, and 79–97% of that is bandwidth. T2 said $0.46.
   - **10,000 CCU spread like OSRS fits on one AX42 even with the TypeScript engine.** T2 said 4–5 boxes.
7. **What it means for AI search:** For an OSRS-style game, CPU per player is already so cheap that finding a faster algorithm saves almost no money for Miles.
   - The README's proposed score ("how many bot players one server can hold before a tick takes longer than its time budget") would mostly hit the 2,046-slot protocol cap.
   - Otherwise it would measure whatever crowd the test invents.
   - Better targets are tick *spikes*: crowd hotspots, login/logout storms and synchronous saves. Measure them as p99/p99.9 tick time on realistic and adversarial traces. These improve game quality. They hardly change the bill.

## 2. Crowds: the worst case

- **RSProt synthetic worst case** (https://github.com/blurite/rsprot/blob/00b2e758ba2326dceffa2d48d9064c66d64e7cc5/BENCHMARKS.md, VERIFIED):
  - Hardware: "AMD Ryzen 3950x (16c 32t, 3.7GHz base, 4.825GHz PBO)", "JDK 1.8.0_402", "a Windows PC with some background noise".
  - Player info: "2046 players are spawned into the world, initialized at random within a 13x13 box." "Every cycle of the benchmark, all 2046 players will **teleport** ... all 2046 players will use public chat to type a 50-character long lorem ipsum text."
  - Single-thread player info: "PlayerInfoBenchmark.benchmark  avgt    3  170.230 ± 7.278  ms/op". Multithreaded: "11.544 ± 0.154  ms/op". "Multithreaded ratio: 0.92x". "1 operation is equal to 1 game cycle."
  - NPC info with "500 NPCs ... in the same box as players": single thread "64.195 ± 3.336  ms/op", multithreaded "10.904 ± 6.120".
  - The NPC notes: "Worth mentioning that the circumstances here are extremely unrealistic, as most of the time you are only rendering 10-20 NPCs at a time".
  - On variance: "On some cycles, if lots of players end up in close proximity of one-another, the pressure will be significantly higher than cycles where they are more evenly spread apart."
  - The benchmark source loops `for (i in 1..<MAX_IDX)` with `MAX_IDX: Int = 2047` and random coordinates in 3200–3213 (protocol/osrs-221/.../PlayerInfoBenchmark.kt, VERIFIED).
  - GUESS: (170.23 + 64.20) / 600 = 39% of one core for update encoding alone in the worst crowd. 170.23 / 600 = 28.4% for player info alone, which matches the topic brief. Per player that is 234.4 / 2,046 = 0.115 ms per player-tick.
- **The protocol bounds crowd cost.** Each observer sees at most about 250 other players, and the view distance shrinks when more are nearby. Engine-TS `src/network/rsbuf/build.ts` (VERIFIED):
  - "static readonly PREFERRED_PLAYERS = 250;" and "static readonly PREFERRED_VIEW_DISTANCE = 15;"
  - "if (this.players.len() >= BuildArea.PREFERRED_PLAYERS) { if (this.viewDistance > 1) { this.viewDistance--;"
  - RSProt: "the view range trying to stick to a number that has 250 high resolution players or less" and "The protocol only supports rendering up to 250 NPCs at a time".
  - So update work grows at most like players × 250, not players². GUESS: 2,046 × 250 ≈ 511,500 observer–entity pairs per tick at worst.
- **Whole-tick crowd estimates (GUESS, arithmetic in `g2_costmodel.py`):**
  - JVM/Rust engine: RSProt info (0.115) + one third of the TypeScript non-update work (0.090 / 3) ≈ 0.145 ms per player-tick. A full crowded world needs ≈ 296 ms, about 49% of one core.
  - TypeScript engine: 2× the RSProt info + the TypeScript non-update work ≈ 0.32 ms per player-tick. That is ≈ 654 ms, which overruns the tick. This fits with the 2004scape developers' focus on the "dense" case and moving update building to Rust.
- **Real crowd incidents (UNVERIFIED, wiki mirror raw/S/Server crash glitch.txt):** "Players were able to crash servers by filling a server with hundreds of accounts and congregating them in Clan Wars multi-combat, then performing server-intensive actions such as [[combat|engaging in combat]] or [[equipment|equipping items]]." (the November 2019 "W501 glitch").
  - GUESS on the mechanism: an equipment change invalidates the cached appearance block, so every observer gets a large new update. RSProt notes that appearance is cached "so once another avatar has been observed, the appearance extended info block is no longer written".
- **Non-crowd spikes matter as much.** rs-sdk's 7.9 s tick came from 185 session takeovers in 16 s (§1.1). The top steady cost was whole-world checkpointing on logouts and GE operations (34.7% of profile samples). These are "storms", and a crowd benchmark would not include them.
- **RuneLite:** I found no player render limit in its code.
  - The API exposes players as `IndexedObjectSet<? extends Player> players();` (runelite-api WorldView.java, commit 27fcc1da).
  - The Entity Hider plugin's description is "Hide players, NPCs, and/or projectiles" (runelite-client .../entityhider/EntityHiderPlugin.java). Its options include "Hide others".
  - That is client-side only and does not reduce server work. The 250-visible cap is on the server side (above).

## 4. Why a world holds ~2,000 players: protocol and policy, not CPU

Protocol ceiling (VERIFIED in four independent reverse-engineered implementations):
- **RSProt** (OSRS protocol library), PlayerInfoProtocol.kt (osrs-242):
  - "The maximum capacity of the player info protocol. ... as the client tends to iterate up to 2048, we cannot often allocate smaller data structures, as that would break the client's logic." `public const val PROTOCOL_CAPACITY: Int = 2048`.
  - "Index 0 is not valid for player info protocol."
  - The login packet sizes initial positions for all other slots: `PLAYER_INFO_BLOCK_SIZE = ((30 + (2046 * 18)) + Byte.SIZE_BITS - 1) ushr 3` (LoginResponse.kt). That is 30 bits for yourself plus 18 bits for each of the 2,046 others.
  - So OSRS has 2,047 usable slots (1–2047).
- **Engine-TS** (2004 protocol): `private static readonly PLAYERS: number = 2047;` and `readonly players: Player[] = new Array(2048);`.
  - The player update stream uses 11-bit indices with 2047 as the end marker: `this.buf.pbit(11, 2047);` (src/network/rsbuf/info.ts).
  - Usable pids are 1–2046.
- **rs-majula**: "`MAX_PLAYERS = 2048`". In the PlayerList table, lower `1`, upper `MAX_PLAYERS-1` = 2047, exclusive.
  - "Players start at pid `1` (pid `0` is reserved" and "11 bits hold 0..2047, exactly `MAX_PLAYERS`".
  - It also writes the sentinel: "emit the sentinel index (`pbit::<11>(2047)` for players".
- **rsmod**: `public class PlayerList : EntityList<Player>(capacity = 2047, slotPadding = 1)`.

Operating cap (Jagex policy; UNVERIFIED, wiki mirror raw/W/World.txt at https://github.com/Kiyogitpy/osrs-wiki-data/blob/8df1f8aea49ba9d919a17a99ebdf39e320f65546/raw/W/World.txt):
- "Each members world allows 2000 players to be connected at once while free-to-play worlds have a limit of 1400 players at once."
  - The cited Mod Ash Discord post (10 November 2025): "The cap's been going up again since then. Didn't really need to be so low. I think it's 1.4k at present, creeping up over time."
- Update history: 9 July 2021, "A Message Regarding Bug Abuse": "The maximum number of players on a [[free-to-play]] world was changed from 2000 to 1000."
- Lost City's friend server uses the same number: `const WORLD_PLAYER_LIMIT = 2000;` (src/server/friend/FriendServer.ts, VERIFIED). The Engine-TS default world config allows `maxConnected: 1000` (src/util/WorldConfig.ts, VERIFIED).

Conclusion (GUESS): the 2,000 members cap sits just under the 11-bit ceiling of 2,046–2,047. The free-world cap was moved to 1,000 for a non-capacity reason and later raised because it "didn't really need to be so low". So the world size is a protocol plus policy choice.
- The measured TypeScript engine handles about 1,900 bots in about 0.5 core at a 600 ms tick, so CPU is not what holds the cap at 2,000 for normal play.
- CPU only bites in crowd plus expensive-action spikes (§2).
- For Miles's own game, the cap is a free design choice: he is not bound by Jagex's protocol.

## 5. Real crowd data: OSRS world populations

### 5.1 Early 2021, 3.33 million samples

Source: https://github.com/ys-lin14/osrs-player-count-scraper (commit 88ab58de, 2021-05-24).
- It scraped https://oldschool.runescape.com/slu every 5 minutes "into a MySQL database using Python, a Raspberry Pi and cron".
- I read `data/world_player_counts.zip` (98 MB CSV) and `data/total_player_counts.csv`.

What the data shows (UNVERIFIED: a third-party scrape of Jagex's page; statistics computed by `worldpop.py`, `worldpop2.py` and `fullcheck.py`):
- 3,326,820 numeric world-samples plus 4,734 empty player cells. 271 worlds, 15,965 snapshots, 2021-01-04 17:25 to 2021-03-01 12:25.
- World-weighted: median 321, p90 837, p99 1,199, mean 397. The highest numeric value was 1,980; none was ≥1,990.
- **An empty player cell means a full world.**
  - In 4,055 snapshots with exactly one empty cell, the site total minus the sum of numeric worlds had a median of 1,994 (p10 1,983, p90 2,000).
  - With two empty cells the median was 3,986.
  - With no empty cell it was 0 (11,776 snapshots).
  - The rs_tracker.py comment in a 2026 tracker makes the same assumption: "# If no number is found, it means the world is full (2000 players)" (https://github.com/UsefulEndymion/osrsplayercount/blob/89f6c95788ee1972516c57731d4938474c3dc848/rs_tracker.py).

## 7. What this means for the AI-search plan and the proposed score

GUESS-level synthesis of the evidence above:

1. **The README's score would mostly measure a cap or an invented crowd.**
   - "How many bot players one server can hold before a tick takes longer than its time budget (0.6 s)": with OSRS-style protocol limits, one world stops at 2,046–2,047 players. Even a TypeScript engine reaches that at only about 50% of the budget with spread-out bots (§1.1).
   - For a box with many worlds, the answer is set by RAM per world and by the crowd/spike model the test author chose. AI search would then optimise for our invented scenario.
2. **What still varies with the algorithm, and what to measure instead:**
   - **p99 / p99.9 tick time in hotspots.** For example, N players within R tiles doing realistic actions (trading, walking, chatting, equipment changes), and an adversarial version (RSProt-style teleport and chat). Report CPU ms per tick, not pass/fail at 600 ms. This is continuous and does not saturate at the cap.
   - **Storm handling.** Login/logout/session-takeover bursts and synchronous saves. rs-sdk's 7.9 s tick from 185 takeovers in 16 s shows a large, real effect. Much of it is fixable by plain engineering (bounding login work per tick, coalescing checkpoints), so the headroom for *AI discovery* specifically is unclear.
   - **Worlds per box (RAM).** This matters only at Jagex scale. AI program search is a poor fit for it compared with ordinary engineering such as shared static data.
   - If a "capacity" score is kept, use a tight budget (T2 proposed p99 ≤ 10% of 600 ms = 60 ms). Then even the TypeScript engine is limited to about 600–700 players (rs-sdk: 623 players → 62 ms with login work). Or lift the 2,046 slot cap in Miles's own protocol, so that one-world crowd capacity becomes the score.
3. **Money:** for an OSRS-like game, compute is about €0.001–0.007 per CCU-month on Hetzner. At 10,000 CCU the whole bill is one or two boxes. The AI-search payoff is game quality (bigger hotspots, no lag at events), an open benchmark, and maybe a bigger-world design. It is not a lower bill for Miles. This agrees with T2 §6, now with sourced inputs.

## 8. Evidence against and caveats

- **Bots are not humans.** rs-sdk bots are scripted agents. Real OSRS players send more input (mouse and camera events), use more content, and gather in different places. Real OSRS also has more NPCs (tens of thousands) and more complex scripts. Real cost per player could be 2–3× higher than the rs-sdk anchor (GUESS). Even then it stays 7–20× below T2's guess.
- **One live anchor.** The normal-play numbers come from one TypeScript server on one machine type, observed for minutes, not hours. The operator says it is "not a controlled load test". The Rust factor (÷2–5) is a GUESS.
- **The crowd numbers are synthetic.** RSProt is a microbenchmark of update encoding only, on a desktop JVM 8. Whole-tick crowd costs are my extrapolation.
- **Jagex's own sizing is unknown.** Jagex may give each world several vCPUs for reasons I could not see: anti-cheat, logging, VM overcommit policy. The W501 crash and the "missed ticks on busiest worlds" note show that real OSRS worlds do hit CPU limits in extreme crowds.
- **World-list data are third-party scrapes.** The 2021 data predate the 2021 F2P cap change and Sailing. Still, the 2026 snapshot looks the same (mean 411).
- **I did not run any engine myself.** This research step asked for sources, and downloaded code must not be run. A one-hour local test of rs-majula's `::bots`/`::localbots` (2,000 static bots, or 2,048 in 15×15 tiles) would give the first direct Rust numbers. It is a good first experiment once Miles approves building.
